Rebuilding graphs from their pieces

Since my first semester I have worked with Professor Sosa-Castillo on a question that links two fields: what does the algebra of reconstructing monomial orders say about the graph reconstruction conjecture? Two manuscripts came out of it. Both figures below are live, and computed in your browser.

Manuscript in preparation · sole author

Reconstruction and the Intersection Number of the Undirected de Bruijn Graph

Delete one vertex from a graph and erase every label: that is a card. The deck is the collection of all cards. The oldest open problem in the area asks whether the deck always remembers the graph.

Conjecture (Kelly–Ulam). Every simple graph on at least three vertices is determined, up to isomorphism, by its deck.

The undirected de Bruijn graph B(k,n) has the kn words of length n as vertices, joined when one word shifts into the other. It is far from regular once loops and parallel edges collapse, and that irregularity is what the proof uses. The manuscript gives an explicit algorithm that rebuilds B(k,n) from its deck: identify a hub card, find the unique neighbourhood that fits, then reconstruct. It also studies the minimum edge-clique covers of B(k,n) and its intersection number.

Manuscript in preparation · with G. Sosa and K. Santo

Strongly Stable Generators of Lexsegment Ideals

Order the monomials of one degree lexicographically and keep an initial stretch: a lexsegment. Lexsegment ideals are the extremal objects of commutative algebra; Macaulay used them to describe every possible Hilbert function.

A lexsegment is always strongly stable: swap any variable for an earlier one and you stay inside. So the whole segment is generated, under those swaps, by a much smaller set of monomials. The manuscript studies those strongly stable generators.

The figure shows the degree-d monomials in x, y, z. Each edge of the lattice is one swap. Drag the slider, or click a monomial to end the segment there, and watch which few monomials (gold) generate everything in maroon.

Power from a pin, not a handle

The sensor reads the reaction at the oarlock, not the hands. Taking moments about the blade for a 374 cm oar with 114 cm inboard gives a lever arm of 0.71 m, so work per stroke is ∫ Fpin · 0.71 dθ.

A reverse-engineered file format

I decoded Peach's undocumented .peach-data binary: one-second frames of 23 channels at 50 Hz, 16-bit samples with a flag bit, angles in sixteenths of a degree.

Built to cost nothing

Exports run 24–67 MB, so analysis happens in a Web Worker in the coach's browser. Traces are stored as gzipped binary, about 3 MB a session. Row-level security makes a cox's note visible only to its rower.

Recursive Polynomial Adaptive Sampling

Fit a curve while looking at as little of the data as possible. Start from ten points, fit a polynomial plus a spline through its residuals, then sample again only where the fit is still wrong. Flat regions are never revisited.

Denoising with nearest-neighbour graphs

Join every point to its nearest neighbour. The graph falls apart into small components; replace each by its centroid and repeat. Noise averages out within a component while the shape of the data survives between them.

It finds the circle, the curve and the branch point. Crossing curves are the known failure case, and the repository keeps that test in.

RouteRight

How should a trading desk split its monthly flow across venues? Volume tiers reward concentration, because reaching a tier re-prices all your flow. Market impact punishes it, because cost grows faster than size. The first is non-convex, the second convex.

RouteRight solves the allocation exactly as a mixed-integer program, with a formal spec and proofs of monotonicity. The figure is the two-venue case with indicative numbers; the engine handles 6–15 venues, token discounts and market-maker programs.
